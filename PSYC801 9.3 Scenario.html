The scenario

Health psychology studies these reciprocal processes across the lifespan. To see what this means in practice, consider two people who both receive the same diagnosis of hypertension.

Both are prescribed the same medication. Both are advised to reduce salt intake and increase physical activity.

Six months later, one person's blood pressure is well controlled. The other remains high.

From a purely biomedical perspective, this difference is puzzling. The diagnosis is the same. The treatment is the same.

From a biopsychosocial and lifespan perspective, the difference is easier to understand.

Yanis

Yanis grew up in a household where exercise was routine and meals were home-prepared. He believes the condition is manageable, feels confident about making lifestyle changes and has supportive family members who walk with him in the evenings. His workplace allows flexible hours, making regular exercise possible.

Mei

Mei has experienced long-term financial strain and shift work since early adulthood. She feels overwhelmed, believes stress is unavoidable and works long hours with limited access to safe spaces for physical activity. Medication is often forgotten during busy shifts. Years of accumulated stress and limited resources shape how she responds to the diagnosis.

Why the differences?

The biology at the point of diagnosis may be similar. The life context is not.

Health psychology asks how early experiences, accumulated stress exposure, beliefs, coping patterns, behaviour and social environments interact over time to shape health outcomes. It examines not just the disease, but also the trajectory that led to it and the conditions that influence what happens next.

Reflect

Take a moment to think about the scenario:

  • What differences do you notice between Yanis and Mei beyond their diagnosis?
  • How might these differences help explain why they experience different health outcomes?
